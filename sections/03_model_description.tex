\section{Model Description}

\subsection{Field Definitions}

The DBS micro-continuum formulation \citep{brinkman1949calculation,soulaine2016micro,
soulaine2024microcontinuum} distinguishes
between three pore-space quantities. The first is the numerical porosity,
$\epsilon_{\mathrm{num}}$, which is retained to regularize transport in nearly
solid voxels. The second is the physical mobile-water fraction, $\theta$,
which defines aqueous accumulation and chemical storage. The third is the
reactive internal surface area per bulk volume, $a_{\mathrm{surf}}$, which
represents unresolved surface available for adsorption and surface
complexation. All transported aqueous species,
together with $\theta$, $\epsilon_{\mathrm{num}}$, $a_{\mathrm{surf}}$, and
the DBS surface fields, are treated as cell-centred variables. The volumetric
flux $\phi$ remains face-centred.

\subsection{Reactive Surface Area Field}
\label{sec:aSurf}

The DBS micro-continuum framework naturally produces three distinct local
material states within a computational cell: a resolved macro-pore (high
$\theta$, negligible grain fraction), a microporous grain (intermediate
$\theta$, significant unresolved grain fraction), and a near-solid or
fully solid region (very low $\theta$). All three must contribute reactive
surface area to the chemistry, but they do so through different geometric
mechanisms. Both contributions are captured in the single cell-centred field
$a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ (units: m$^2\,\mathrm{m}^{-3}_{\mathrm{bulk}}$), which is
the sum of two terms:
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{surf}} = a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{interface}} +
a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{intrinsic}}.
\label{eq:asurf}
\end{equation}

The first term, $a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{interface}}$, is computed
geometrically from the segmented voxel image by counting all fluid--solid
face adjacencies at the fine-image scale. Within each coarse finite-volume
cell containing $N_{\mathrm{vox}} = p \times q \times r$ fine voxels of
resolution $\Delta x$, let $N_{\mathrm{fs}}$ denote the number of voxel
face pairs where one voxel belongs to a fluid phase and its neighbour belongs
to a solid or micropore phase. The volumetric interface density is
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{interface}}
= \frac{N_{\mathrm{fs}}}{N_{\mathrm{vox}}\,\Delta x}.
\label{eq:asurf_interface}
\end{equation}
This is the standard specific surface area estimator from binary image
analysis: each counted face contributes area $\Delta x^2$ to the numerator
while the coarse cell volume is $N_{\mathrm{vox}}\,\Delta x^3$.

The second term, $a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{intrinsic}}$, is a
user-specified material parameter, analogous to the permeability field. A
volumetric surface density $s_k$ (units: m$^2$\,m$^{-3}$) characteristic of
phase $k$ (for example the BET surface area of a carbonate micropore grain)
is prescribed for each image label, and the intrinsic contribution is
assembled by weighting each phase density by its local volume fraction $f_k$
within the coarse cell:
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{intrinsic}} = \sum_k f_k\,s_k.
\label{eq:asurf_intrinsic}
\end{equation}
Both fields are pre-computed from the segmented image before the flow
simulation begins and are held constant throughout the reactive-transport
calculation.

Both contributions enter the geochemical module through identical treatment.
The PHREEQC reaction engine requires surface area expressed per litre of
water, not per bulk cell volume. The conversion is
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{water}} = \frac{a_{\mathrm{surf}}}{10^{3}\,\theta},
\label{eq:asurf_water}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the local mobile-water fraction (volume of water per bulk
volume) and the factor $10^3$ converts cubic metres to litres. This
normalization correctly accounts for the heterogeneous distribution of water:
in a microporous cell with small $\theta$, the same bulk surface area density
is concentrated in a proportionally smaller water volume, yielding a higher
effective surface-to-water ratio. In a fully open macro-pore cell ($\theta
\approx 1$) the factor reduces to a simple unit conversion.

Critically, the numerical porosity $\epsilon_{\mathrm{num}}$ does not appear
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:asurf_water}. Its role is confined to the DBS momentum
equation, where it regularizes the Brinkman term in near-solid cells. All
chemical accumulation and surface coupling is gated on $\theta$ alone, so
numerical regularization and geochemical coupling are fully decoupled. Cells
in which $\theta$ falls below a small threshold are treated as chemically
inactive and their surface terms are suppressed.

\subsection{Conservative Aqueous Transport}

For each aqueous species with concentration $C_i$, the conservative DBS
transport equation is written as
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial (\theta C_i)}{\partial t}
+ \nabla \cdot (\phi C_i)
- \nabla \cdot (\theta D_i \nabla C_i) = 0,
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the volumetric flux field obtained from a prior
Darcy--Brinkman--Stokes flow solve, and $D_i$ is the diffusivity assigned to
species $i$. In fully resolved macro-pore cells ($\theta \approx 1$), $D_i$
is the free molecular diffusivity. In cells that represent microporous
material ($0 < \theta < 1$), the unresolved pore structure introduces
tortuosity; in that case $D_i$ may be replaced by an effective diffusivity
$D_i^{\mathrm{eff}} = D_i / \tau$, where $\tau \geq 1$ is the tortuosity
factor of the micropore network. In either case, the $\theta$ factor
multiplying $D_i$ in the diffusion term already accounts for the reduced
water volume available for transport. There is no chemistry source term in
this equation: reactions are handled by the subsequent operator-split
PHREEQC step and do not appear as an explicit right-hand side here.

This form is the correct mobile-water balance. The numerical porosity
$\epsilon_{\mathrm{num}}$ is not used as a chemistry storage term unless an
explicit modeling choice is made to set $\theta = \epsilon_{\mathrm{num}}$.

For a vector of aqueous species $\bm{C}$, the transport step can be written in
compact form as
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial (\theta \bm{C})}{\partial t}
+ \nabla \cdot (\phi \bm{C})
- \nabla \cdot (\theta \bm{D} \nabla \bm{C}) = \bm{0},
\end{equation}
which makes the conservative structure explicit before any chemistry update is
applied.

\subsection{Cell-Based Surface Inventory}

For the unresolved DBS surface, the conservative local inventory is expressed
in the current solver field variables as
\begin{equation}
M = \theta C + \frac{a_{\mathrm{surf}}}{\theta}\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}},
\label{eq:inventory}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}}$ is the cell-centred surface-state field
written by the implementation. It is related to the standard surface
concentration $\Gamma$ (mol\,m$^{-2}$ of solid surface) via the
PHREEQC water-volume convention: PHREEQC expresses adsorbed quantities per
litre of water, which after unit conversion gives a field quantity whose
ratio to the aqueous concentration has units of metres. Specifically, the
standard Henry coefficient $K_s = \Gamma / C$ (units: m) satisfies
$\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}} = K_s\,\theta\,C$, so
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}} = \Gamma\,\theta
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\Gamma = \frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}}}{\theta},
\label{eq:Gamma_conversion}
\end{equation}
and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}}$ has effective units mol\,m$^{-2}$ (the
$\theta$ factor is dimensionless). The $a_{\mathrm{surf}}/\theta$ factor in
the adsorbed inventory then follows from substituting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Gamma_conversion} into the standard expression
$\Gamma \times a_{\mathrm{surf}}$:
\begin{equation*}
\Gamma \times a_{\mathrm{surf}}
= \frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}}}{\theta} \times a_{\mathrm{surf}}
= \frac{a_{\mathrm{surf}}}{\theta}\,\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}},
\end{equation*}
recovering the adsorbed term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:inventory}.
Physically, a microporous cell with small $\theta$ holds less water but
exposes more surface per unit water volume; the two effects combine to give
the $\theta^{-1}$ dependence of the stored adsorbed amount per bulk volume.

This field-level expression is the appropriate quantity for analytical
comparisons and for benchmark inventory checks. For multiple surface species,
the local bulk inventory is obtained by summing the relevant aqueous and
adsorbed contributions. Single-species notation is used below only for clarity;
all reported totals are evaluated in the actual implemented field variables.

\subsection{Local Chemistry Coupling}

The chemical update is local to each cell. Over one transport--chemistry
cycle, the transport step first advances the aqueous species in conservative
finite-volume form. The updated cell state, together with the mobile-water
fraction and cell-based surface state, is then mapped into PHREEQC
\citep{parkhurst2013phreeqc} via the PhreeqcRM interface
\citep{parkhurst2015phreeqcrm}. PHREEQC solves the local chemistry under the
assumption of \emph{thermodynamic equilibrium}: within each timestep, surface
complexation reactions are treated as instantaneous relative to transport.
This is appropriate when the equilibration timescale of the surface reactions
is short compared with the transport timescale (P\'{e}clet- and
Damk\"{o}hler-number regime), which holds for the strongly sorbing systems
considered here. Kinetic surface reactions can in principle be handled by
the same interface but are not used in the present work.

The baseline production method uses a first-order Lie splitting
\citep{carrayrou2004operator}. The splitting introduces a global error of
$\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)$ in the operator-split solution; the local error over
one step is $\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$. In practice, provided the timestep is
chosen so that advection does not move a concentration front through more
than one cell per step (i.e.\ the CFL number is at most $O(1)$), the
splitting error remains comparable to the spatial discretisation error and
does not dominate. Higher-order splitting is intentionally deferred until
the conservative baseline is fully established.

At the algorithmic level, one timestep of size $\Delta t$ is:
\begin{align}
\bm{C}^{n+1/2} &= \mathcal{T}_{\Delta t}(\bm{C}^{n}, \theta, \phi, \bm{D}),\\
(\bm{C}^{n+1}, \bm{\Gamma}^{n+1}) &=
\mathcal{R}_{\Delta t}(\bm{C}^{n+1/2}, \bm{\Gamma}^{n}, \theta, a_{\mathrm{surf}}),
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{T}_{\Delta t}$ is the conservative finite-volume transport
operator (advancing $\theta\bm{C}$ in DBS form) and $\mathcal{R}_{\Delta t}$
is the local PHREEQC equilibrium operator that updates both aqueous and
surface states simultaneously within each cell.

\subsection{Analytical Dilute Limit}

In the dilute linear-equilibrium limit, surface complexation reduces to a
Henry-type isotherm. The standard Henry coefficient is $K_s = \Gamma / C$
(units: m), the ratio of surface concentration $\Gamma$ (mol\,m$^{-2}$) to
aqueous concentration $C$ (mol\,m$^{-3}$). Using the field-storage
convention $\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}} = \Gamma\,\theta$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:Gamma_conversion}), the field-level linear equilibrium reads
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}} = K_{s,\mathrm{field}}\,C,
\qquad K_{s,\mathrm{field}} = K_s\,\theta \quad [\mathrm{m}].
\end{equation}
In practice, $K_{s,\mathrm{field}}$ is not prescribed directly but is
extracted numerically from a pair of batch equilibrations at two different
aqueous concentrations:
$K_{s,\mathrm{field}} = \Delta\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}} / \Delta C$.
This avoids any ambiguity in the PHREEQC database-to-field unit mapping and
provides the coefficient that enters the retardation formula directly.

Substituting into the inventory Eq.~\eqref{eq:inventory} and taking the
time derivative yields the retarded 1D transport equation
\begin{equation}
\left(
\theta + \frac{a_{\mathrm{surf}}}{\theta}K_{s,\mathrm{field}}
\right)\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}
+ \frac{\partial (\phi C)}{\partial x} = 0.
\end{equation}
The corresponding retardation factor is
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{field}} = 1 + \frac{a_{\mathrm{surf}}\,K_{s,\mathrm{field}}}{\theta^2}
= 1 + \frac{a_{\mathrm{surf}}\,K_s}{\theta},
\label{eq:retardation}
\end{equation}
where the second form recovers the standard result written in terms of $K_s$
and $\theta$. Both $a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ and $\theta$ can vary spatially, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:retardation} also gives the local retardation at any point in
a heterogeneous domain. The analytical advection-diffusion profile against
which the numerical results are compared follows \citet{ogata1961solution},
with the pore velocity $u/\theta$ replaced by $u/(\theta R_{\mathrm{field}})$
and the diffusivity replaced by $D/R_{\mathrm{field}}$.

\subsection{Validation Requirements}

The validation programme presented in this paper is designed to establish four
criteria. First, the formulation must reduce to simpler known limits. Second,
the local surface-coupled chemistry must conserve the intended field-level
inventory. Third, the formulation must reproduce the dilute analytical
retardation limit. Fourth, the same formulation must remain applicable as the
geometric complexity increases from 1D to image-based 2D and 3D domains.
