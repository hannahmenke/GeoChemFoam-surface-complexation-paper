\section{Introduction}

Reactive transport in porous media is controlled not only by advection and
diffusion, but also by chemical exchange between the flowing aqueous phase and
the solid matrix. In many subsurface systems, this exchange is strongly
mediated by surface complexation, ion adsorption, and fast local equilibrium
reactions that retard dissolved species, reshape concentration fronts, and
change the apparent accessibility of pore space. These effects are especially
important in multi-scale rocks that combine open macropores with microporous
matrix regions, because the same image may contain fast transport pathways,
slow exchange domains, and large unresolved internal reactive area.

Classical Darcy-scale reactive transport models can represent adsorption and
surface complexation through effective source terms or equilibrium closures,
and they remain indispensable for large-domain prediction. However, these
models usually require constitutive upscaling assumptions for both transport
and reactive surface area, and they do not directly expose how local geometry
controls the spatial distribution of dissolved and adsorbed species. At the
other extreme, fully resolved pore-scale models can represent grain surfaces
and local transport paths explicitly, but they become expensive quickly and
they are fundamentally limited by image resolution: once internal micro-scale
surface area falls below the resolved mesh scale, a purely boundary-resolved
description ceases to be faithful to the physical geometry. Pore-network and
other reduced pore-scale models provide an important middle ground, but they
still depend on geometric idealization and do not naturally deliver the same
voxel-by-voxel field description used in DBS image-based simulations.

This gap is particularly important for modern image-based simulations of
carbonate and other multi-scale porous media. Segmented images often separate
macropore, microporous, and nearly solid regions, but the reactive internal
surface of the microporous phase is not equivalent to the explicitly meshed
boundary of the coarse computational cells. If that internal area is ignored,
reactive uptake is under-predicted. If it is inserted as an ad hoc volumetric
sink, conservation and interpretation can become unclear. A useful
intermediate-scale method therefore needs to do two things at once: preserve a
conservative transport balance in the mobile fluid, and represent unresolved
surface chemistry as a physically interpretable cell-scale inventory.

PHREEQC and its reaction-module form, PhreeqcRM, provide a robust chemical
engine for this problem because they already support aqueous speciation,
surface complexation, equilibrium and kinetic reactions, and operator-split
coupling to transport simulators. The practical challenge is not the chemical
model itself, but how to embed it into a transport formulation that is
consistent with unresolved image-based geometry. Existing PHREEQC-coupled
transport workflows are well suited to structured-grid continuum models and to
operator-split multi-component solvers, but they do not automatically provide
a mathematically clean representation of cell-based internal surface area in a
DBS-style pore-structure model. In particular, when unresolved reactive area
is treated as a cell property rather than an explicit mesh boundary, the
choice of storage variable, porosity variable, and surface-state variable
becomes central.

This is also the key gap relative to earlier resolved-surface workflows within
GeoChemFoam itself. Existing PHREEQC-coupled cases in the code base already
support reactive transport and surface complexation when the reactive surface
coincides with an explicit mesh boundary. That capability is useful and
remains valid, but it does not address the present case: in DBS image-based
simulations, the dominant reactive area may live inside the coarse cell as
unresolved internal surface, particularly in the microporous phase. The
present work therefore requires a new cell-based formulation rather than a
direct reuse of the resolved-boundary implementation.

The motivating use case for this work is therefore not merely ``adding
PHREEQC'' to an existing solver. The real objective is to construct a
DBS-compatible reactive transport formulation in which:
\begin{itemize}
\item aqueous transport is written for the physical mobile-water fraction,
\item unresolved reactive surface area is represented as a cell-based
inventory,
\item local PHREEQC chemistry transfers mass conservatively between aqueous
and adsorbed states,
\item the implementation does not rely on non-conservative numerical repairs
such as clipping or heuristic source redistribution.
\end{itemize}

These requirements are stricter than a simple source-term augmentation. They
force an explicit distinction between the numerical DBS porosity used for
stable transport in nearly solid voxels and the physically mobile water
fraction used for chemistry. They also force a clear statement of where the
surface inventory lives: in the present work, it is cell-centered and tied to
an unresolved internal surface-area density rather than reconstructed on mesh
faces. This is the key modeling choice that allows the method to act on
microporous matrix regions instead of only on explicitly resolved pore--grain
boundaries.

The solver developed here is \texttt{reactiveScalarTransportDBSFoam}. It
couples conservative DBS transport to PHREEQC through a staged implementation
path, beginning with validated reductions and adding cell-based surface
complexation only after the simpler limits have been shown to behave
correctly. This staged approach is deliberate: it makes each major reduction
path auditable and avoids obscuring conceptual errors behind a large,
monolithic solver change.

Accordingly, the manuscript is organized as a validation ladder rather than a
single end-state demonstration. The paper first states the field variables and
the conservative transport/reaction balances, then describes the numerical
implementation, and then validates the method in progressively richer settings:
a 1D analytical retardation limit, a transparent 2D image-based case, a simple
3D image-based case, and finally a larger multi-scale 3D porous-medium
demonstration. References to prior pore-scale, Darcy-scale, and
PHREEQC-coupled reactive transport literature will be added in the final
manuscript, but the draft is already written to make clear which specific gap
this method is intended to fill: conservative unresolved surface complexation
in image-based DBS reactive transport.
