\section{Introduction}

Reactive transport in porous media is controlled not only by advection and
diffusion, but also by chemical exchange between the flowing aqueous phase and
the solid matrix. In many subsurface systems, this exchange is strongly
mediated by surface complexation, ion adsorption, and fast local equilibrium
reactions that retard dissolved species, reshape concentration fronts, and
change the apparent accessibility of pore space \citep{dzombak1990surface}.
These effects are especially important in multi-scale rocks that combine open
macropores with microporous matrix regions, because the same image may contain
fast transport pathways, slow exchange domains, and large unresolved internal
reactive area \citep{menke2021upscaling}.

Classical Darcy-scale reactive transport models can represent adsorption and
surface complexation through effective source terms or equilibrium closures,
and they remain indispensable for large-domain prediction
\citep{steefel1990kinetic,steefel1994coupled,steefel2015reactive,mayer2002multicomponent,xu2006toughreact}. However, these models usually
require constitutive upscaling assumptions for both transport and reactive
surface area, and they do not directly expose how local geometry controls the
spatial distribution of dissolved and adsorbed species. At the other extreme,
fully resolved pore-scale models can represent grain surfaces and local
transport paths explicitly \citep{molins2021simulation,molins2015reactive,yoon2015lattice,kang2007lattice}, but they become
expensive quickly and they are fundamentally limited by image resolution: once
internal micro-scale surface area falls below the resolved mesh scale, a
purely boundary-resolved description ceases to be faithful to the physical
geometry. Pore-network and other reduced pore-scale models provide an important
middle ground \citep{blunt2001flow}, but they still depend on geometric
idealization and do not naturally deliver the same voxel-by-voxel field
description used in DBS image-based simulations.

This gap is particularly important for modern image-based simulations of
carbonate and other multi-scale porous media \citep{menke2015dynamic,
menke2018fourd,menke2021upscaling,wildenschild2013xray,blunt2013porescale}. Segmented images often separate macropore,
microporous, and nearly solid regions, but the reactive internal surface of the
microporous phase is not equivalent to the explicitly meshed boundary of the
coarse computational cells. If that internal area is ignored, reactive uptake
is under-predicted. If it is inserted as an ad hoc volumetric sink, conservation
and interpretation can become unclear. A useful intermediate-scale method
therefore needs to do two things at once: preserve a conservative transport
balance in the mobile fluid, and represent unresolved surface chemistry as a
physically interpretable cell-scale inventory.

PHREEQC and its reaction-module form, PhreeqcRM, provide a robust chemical
engine for this problem because they already support aqueous speciation,
surface complexation, equilibrium and kinetic reactions, and operator-split
coupling to transport simulators \citep{parkhurst2013phreeqc,
parkhurst2015phreeqcrm,charlton2011modules}. The practical challenge is not the chemical model
itself, but how to embed it into a transport formulation that is consistent
with unresolved image-based geometry. Existing PHREEQC-coupled transport
workflows are well suited to structured-grid continuum models and to
operator-split multi-component solvers \citep{jyoti2021validation}, but they do not automatically provide a
mathematically clean representation of cell-based internal surface area in a
DBS-style pore-structure model. In particular, when unresolved reactive area
is treated as a cell property rather than an explicit mesh boundary, the
choice of storage variable, porosity variable, and surface-state variable
becomes central.

Several previous approaches have addressed parts of this problem, but none
simultaneously satisfies all the requirements described above. At the
Darcy scale, codes such as those reviewed by \citet{steefel2015reactive} and
the foundational work of \citet{lichtner1985continuum} represent surface
complexation as a bulk sorption coefficient or equilibrium closure applied to
the continuum volume, which cannot capture the spatial heterogeneity of surface
loading visible in image-based data \citep{battiato2011applicability}. Fully resolved pore-scale methods,
including direct numerical simulation \citep{molins2012investigation,starchenko2018development} and
explicit-boundary DBS approaches \citep{maes2018direct,maes2021geochemfoam,soulaine2017mineral},
locate reactive uptake at the meshed grain surface: this is appropriate when
the dominant surface area coincides with the resolved pore--grain interface but
fails for microporous phases where the internal surface area is sub-grid by
construction. Pore-network models offer geometric
flexibility but depend on idealized throat-and-pore representations that do
not readily accommodate cell-based unresolved surface inventories on segmented
images. The closest existing approach to the present work is
\texttt{porousMedia4Foam} \citep{soulaine2021porousmedia4foam,
pavuluri2022reactive}, which couples a micro-continuum OpenFOAM solver with
PHREEQC at pore, hybrid, and Darcy scales. That framework couples chemistry
through kinetic or equilibrium flux conditions applied at the diffuse
fluid-solid interface, inheriting the same limitation as the fully resolved
methods: the reactive surface must overlap with the resolved computational
boundary, and no explicit distinction is drawn between the numerical DBS
porosity used for transport regularization and the physical mobile-water
fraction used for chemistry. The present work addresses this gap directly.

This is also the key gap relative to earlier resolved-surface workflows within
GeoChemFoam itself \citep{maes2022heat,maes2022vos}.
Existing PHREEQC-coupled cases in the code base already support reactive
transport and surface complexation when the reactive surface coincides with an
explicit mesh boundary. That capability is useful and remains valid, but it
does not address the present case: in DBS image-based simulations, the dominant
reactive area may live inside the coarse cell as unresolved internal surface,
particularly in the microporous phase. The present work therefore requires a
new cell-based formulation rather than a direct reuse of the
resolved-boundary implementation.

The objective of this work is therefore not simply to augment an existing
solver with a chemical backend. The real objective is to construct a
DBS-compatible reactive transport formulation in which aqueous transport is
written for the physical mobile-water fraction; unresolved reactive surface
area is represented as a cell-based inventory; local PHREEQC chemistry
transfers mass conservatively between aqueous and adsorbed states; and the
implementation does not rely on non-conservative numerical repairs such as
clipping or heuristic source redistribution.

These requirements are stricter than a simple source-term augmentation. They
force an explicit distinction between the numerical DBS porosity used for
stable transport in nearly solid voxels \citep{soulaine2016micro} and the
physically mobile water fraction used for chemistry. They also force a clear
statement of where the surface inventory lives: in the present work, it is
cell-centered and tied to an unresolved internal surface-area density rather
than reconstructed on mesh faces. This is the key modeling choice that allows
the method to act on microporous matrix regions instead of only on explicitly
resolved pore--grain boundaries.

The solver presented here, \texttt{reactiveScalarTransportDBSFoam}, couples
conservative DBS transport to PHREEQC-based surface complexation at the
cell scale. The manuscript first states the field variables and the
conservative transport/reaction balances, then describes the numerical
implementation. Validation is carried out against a 1D analytical retardation
solution, which provides a closed-form reference for the coupled
advection--diffusion--sorption system. The method is then applied to three
image-based test cases of increasing geometric complexity to demonstrate applicability across realistic
pore geometries: a 2D fracture network (Pb$^{2+}$ on HFO, motivated by
heavy-metal remediation in fractured aquifers and permeable reactive
barriers), a 3D Kelvin-cell geometry (competitive Zn$^{2+}$/Ca$^{2+}$
sorption, representative of trace-metal contamination in Ca-bearing
groundwater), and a 3D Estaillades limestone (Sr$^{2+}$ DGR scenario for
radionuclide migration in nuclear waste repositories).
