\section{Discussion}

The formulation separates two material properties that are often conflated in
image-based reactive transport. The mobile-water fraction $\theta$ defines
aqueous storage and transport, whereas $a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ defines surface
capacity. Their combination in the inventory
$\theta C+a_{\mathrm{surf}}\Gamma$ makes the mass transferred by PHREEQC
explicit on a bulk-volume basis. Reactions remain local to a cell and do not
require an equivalent face flux or redistributed source.

The validation sequence identifies which part of this construction each
comparison can support. Analytical and batch tests constrain conservative
coupling. Controlled interfaces and resolved-wall image calculations constrain
only the image-scale area term. The body-fitted reference is useful where a
tractable wall can be meshed, but it has different wall placement and cannot
represent intrinsic area within a coarse microporous cell. Agreement at the
near-percent level in area and sorbed inventory therefore supports the mapped
interface contribution without implying pointwise geometric identity or
validating an arbitrary intrinsic-area value.

That hierarchy also separates a numerical verification from a physical
upscaling assumption. The one-dimensional and batch cases establish that the
implemented variables exchange mass consistently. The geometric tests establish
that the visible interface can be reconstructed accurately in the tested image
settings. Neither result measures the internal pore-wall area of a microporous
phase or proves that every part of that area equilibrates with a passing front.
Those properties enter as physical inputs and assumptions, which should be
constrained independently for each material and transport regime.

The applications show why the distinction matters. In the fracture network,
macropores deliver Pb rapidly while matrix cells provide the storage capacity.
In Estaillades limestone, connected microporosity distributes Sr uptake and
produces a Ca desorption wave through competition for a fixed surface
inventory. These mechanisms cannot be inferred from dissolved concentration
alone; paired aqueous and surface fields reveal whether delayed transport is
caused by accessibility, capacity, or competitive exchange.

This representation is particularly useful when a segmented image contains
several pore classes. A single porosity-scaled reaction term would make it hard
to identify whether uptake changes because water volume, surface density, or
site chemistry has changed. Here those contributions remain explicit. The
approach can consequently support controlled sensitivity studies of image
labels, intrinsic-area estimates, or surface models, provided that each changed
quantity is stated on the same bulk-volume basis and tested against the paired
inventories.

The intrinsic density remains the principal physical input uncertainty. It
must be constrained by finer imaging or measurements such as BET surface area,
then converted to the bulk-volume basis used here. A local equilibrium cell
also assumes that this internal area is accessible over the resolved transport
timestep. Advective bypass or slow matrix diffusion can violate that
assumption even when total area is known. The short reconstruction sensitivity
reported here does not replace a full-horizon resolved-to-upscaled transport
validation.

Other limits are numerical and chemical. First-order upwind transport and Lie
splitting introduce spatial and timestep errors; sharper fronts or stiffer
systems require resolution studies and potentially higher-order splitting.
The chemistry mask removes near-solid cells at a fixed threshold, whose
influence on cost and inventory should be tested for new geometries. Surface
area is time-independent, reactions are at equilibrium, and the examples do
not include dissolution, precipitation, kinetic surface reactions, or evolving
porosity. Finally, reproducible restart requires the finite-volume fields and
their matched PHREEQCRM checkpoints. These restrictions define the present
scope and the benchmarks required before extension to evolving surfaces or
kinetic systems.
