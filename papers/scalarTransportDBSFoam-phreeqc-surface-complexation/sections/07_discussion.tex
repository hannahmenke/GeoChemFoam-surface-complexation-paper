\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Formulation Choices and Conservative Structure}

The central modeling choice in this work is the use of the physical
mobile-water fraction $\theta$ as the chemistry accumulation variable rather
than the numerical DBS porosity $\epsilon_{\mathrm{num}}$. This distinction
is not merely notational. In the DBS micro-continuum approach, the numerical
porosity is retained at a finite minimum value throughout the solid skeleton
to regularize the Brinkman drag term and stabilize transport in nearly solid
voxels. Using $\epsilon_{\mathrm{num}}$ directly as a chemical storage term
would therefore dilute aqueous concentrations into artificial near-solid
control volumes introduced only for numerical regularization, and would inflate
the apparent retardation. The formulation presented here avoids this by
defining the mobile-water fraction separately from the
numerical porosity and by using $\theta$ consistently in both the transport
accumulation term and the PHREEQC input. This separation is the core modeling
decision of the paper: flow regularization is allowed to remain a DBS device,
while chemistry is tied only to physically mobile water and to the local
cell-based surface inventory.

This approach extends earlier micro-continuum reactive transport formulations
to the case where the reactive surface area is an unresolved cell property
rather than an explicitly meshed boundary. In existing pore-scale DBS solvers,
including \nolinkurl{porousMedia4Foam} and earlier GeoChemFoam solvers,
reactive uptake is typically imposed as a flux condition on the diffuse
fluid-solid interface. This requires the reactive surface to coincide with the
resolved image voxel boundary, which is a good approximation when the dominant
reactive area is at the macropore--grain contact. In multi-scale carbonate
images, however, the microporous phase contains large internal reactive area
that cannot be recovered from the coarse-cell boundary alone. The present
cell-based formulation addresses this gap by representing the unresolved
micropore surface as an intrinsic volumetric density
$a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{intrinsic}}$, scaled by the local phase fraction
and coupled to PHREEQC as a cell-level property.

A consequence of this design is that the conservation structure is enforced at
the cell level rather than through a face flux. The surface inventory
$M = \theta C + (a_{\mathrm{surf}}/\theta)\,\Gamma_{\mathrm{field}}$ is
updated locally by PHREEQC at each timestep, and the aqueous transport step is
conservative in the standard finite-volume sense. There is no explicit
surface-flux term on the cell face that could introduce an additional
non-conservation error. This cell-local coupling is simpler and more
transparent than an implicit composite source approach, and it is directly
auditable through the 1D analytical benchmark.

\subsection{Geometric Scalability}

The revised validation sequence deliberately separates conservative coupling,
surface-area geometry, and microporosity. In the 1D case, the retardation
factor can be derived analytically and matched at sub-percent bulk accuracy,
so the transport--chemistry coupling can be checked without geometric
ambiguity. The controlled sphere and circular-post benchmarks then isolate the
geometry question and show whether the image-derived interface-area field
approaches the intended smooth surface in both 3D and quasi-2D. The
solid-grain micromodel comparisons extend that geometric check to a reactive
image benchmark, and the later porous-media cases add the intrinsic micropore
contribution as a separate modelling layer. This ordering is important because
it prevents uncertainty in the intrinsic micropore density from being confused
with uncertainty in the base interface-area construction.

The transition from 2D to 3D introduces qualitatively different transport paths
and surface-loading distributions. In the 2D case, the dissolved front advances
approximately uniformly along the fracture channels, and the adsorbed-calcium
field follows the fracture geometry. In the 3D cases, the tortuosity of the
pore network produces a more dispersed front and a more heterogeneous
surface-loading pattern. This spatial heterogeneity in surface uptake is
physically important for predicting effective retardation factors at the
continuum scale, and it can only be captured by an image-based simulation that
resolves the local connectivity.

\subsection{Computational Cost and Scaling}

The chemistry fraction of 36--55\% observed in the 1D benchmark sensitivity
study reflects the fact that PHREEQC is called once per cell per timestep, with
a full speciation and surface equilibration for each cell. This is the expected
cost model for a PHREEQC-coupled operator-split solver: the chemistry overhead
depends primarily on the number of active cells, the complexity of the
geochemical system, and the frequency of the chemistry call. For the simple
calcium surface-complexation system used here, the per-cell chemistry time is
small relative to transport, but it accumulates over the full cell count.

In the Estaillades case, the total wall-clock time to $t = 50$ is approximately
$25$~s on eight MPI ranks, of which $\approx 7$~s is attributed to chemistry.
This is already a tractable cost for a $50^3$ coarse grid. For larger grids or
more complex geochemical systems, cell masking (as applied here for
$\theta \le 10^{-3}$) is an important mechanism for reducing the chemistry cost
by eliminating near-solid cells that carry no physical aqueous inventory. The
present paper keeps that threshold fixed across all examples for consistency;
its influence on accuracy and cost deserves a dedicated sensitivity study.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Several limitations of the current implementation should be acknowledged.

First, the intrinsic micropore surface density
$a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{intrinsic}}$ is supplied as a user input per image
phase rather than inferred directly from the segmented image. The method
therefore does not prescribe an image-to-upscaled-surface workflow. In
practice, this quantity must be estimated from complementary measurements such
as nitrogen BET
adsorption, mercury intrusion porosimetry, or secondary image analysis at a
finer resolution \citep{noiriel2009changes}. The formulation accommodates any value of this parameter, but
the physical accuracy of the results depends on how well it reflects the true
internal reactive area of the microporous phase. After the surface-area
validation steps described above, this intrinsic density becomes the leading
remaining physical uncertainty in microporous applications. Future work will
therefore investigate methods for systematically linking the image-derived
phase fractions to measured or computed internal surface-area densities, and
for quantifying sensitivity to this input.

Second, the upscaled DBS representation assumes that unresolved matrix
properties can be represented as local cell-averaged quantities. This is most
appropriate when diffusive exchange between the resolved pore space and the
unresolved matrix is fast on the advective residence time. Under those
conditions, intrinsic surface area behaves as a locally accessible reactive
reservoir. At larger P\'{e}clet number, however, advective bypass and finite
matrix-exchange times can make only part of the intrinsic surface area
accessible to a passing concentration front. Sorption errors in this regime
therefore reflect not only uncertainty in the amount of surface area, but also
uncertainty in its temporal accessibility. This limitation is inherent to any
local equilibrium upscaling of unresolved reactive matrix, and motivates
Pe-resolved benchmark comparisons when applying the method to strongly
advective systems.

Third, the current implementation uses a first-order Lie splitting between
transport and chemistry. This is known to introduce splitting error that scales
with the timestep and the stiffness of the reaction terms. For the
surface-complexation system used here, the splitting error is acceptable at the
validated timesteps, but it may become significant for faster reactions or
stiffer geochemical systems. A second-order Strang splitting
\citep{strang1968construction} is a natural extension, but the present work
uses Lie splitting deliberately so that the conservative transport operator and
the local PHREEQC chemistry operator can be validated first in their simplest
combined form.

Finally, the present demonstrations use fixed surface area and equilibrium
surface complexation. They do not yet cover evolving reactive area, mineral
dissolution/precipitation feedbacks, or kinetic surface reactions. Those
extensions are compatible with the same cell-based inventory structure, but
they will require additional benchmark design to separate transport error,
surface-area evolution, and reaction-model uncertainty.
