\section{Introduction}

Surface complexation can retard dissolved solutes, reshape concentration
fronts, and couple transport to the chemical accessibility of the solid phase
\citep{dzombak1990surface}. The coupling is particularly consequential in
multiscale rocks and sorbent media, where connected macropores transmit solute
while microporous regions provide much of the reactive area
\citep{menke2021upscaling}. This combination occurs in contaminant retention,
critical-mineral recovery, and radionuclide transport
\citep{blowes2000treatment,reich2022lithium,dewindt2019reactive}. Predictive
models must therefore distinguish the water volume that transports solute from
the surface area that stores adsorbed species.

Darcy-scale reactive-transport models represent adsorption through continuum
closures and remain essential for field-scale prediction
\citep{steefel2015reactive,mayer2002multicomponent}. Fully resolved pore-scale
models instead locate reactions on explicitly meshed grain surfaces
\citep{molins2015reactive,maes2021geochemfoam}. Neither description alone is
suited to segmented images containing both resolved flow paths and unresolved
internal porosity. A boundary-resolved model cannot recover surface below the
image resolution, whereas an unspecified volumetric sink obscures the storage
capacity and its mass-balance interpretation.

Darcy--Brinkman--Stokes (DBS) micro-continuum models bridge pore and continuum
descriptions by assigning porosity and permeability to each image cell
\citep{soulaine2016micro}. Existing DBS reactive-transport formulations are
well suited to reactions at a resolved or diffuse pore--grain interface
\citep{maes2018direct,soulaine2021porousmedia4foam}. In a microporous image
phase, however, the dominant surface may lie inside the cell rather than on
that interface. Its area must be introduced as an independent material
property while retaining conservative transport in the mobile water.

This requirement is more restrictive than adding a reaction-rate term to a
transport equation. The aqueous inventory scales with mobile-water volume,
whereas the surface inventory scales with reactive area. A microporous cell can
therefore have a small mobile-water fraction and a large internal area without
either quantity being derived from the other. The numerical representation
must preserve that independence, express both inventories on a common bulk
volume, and suppress chemistry only where the mobile-water volume is too small
to define a meaningful solution. These choices determine both conservation and
the physical interpretation of an image-based result.

PHREEQC and PhreeqcRM provide aqueous speciation, surface complexation, and a
reaction-module interface for operator-split transport solvers
\citep{parkhurst2013phreeqc,parkhurst2015phreeqcrm}. The outstanding modelling
question is how to map their local solution and surface states onto a DBS cell
without conflating numerical transport regularisation, physical mobile-water
volume, and reactive area. We address this question with a cell-centred
surface inventory coupled to a conservative finite-volume aqueous balance.
The reactive-area field combines image-scale interface area with an intrinsic
sub-voxel contribution assigned by material phase; PHREEQC then transfers mass
locally between aqueous and adsorbed states without heuristic source
redistribution.

The formulation is intended for segmented images in which resolved pores,
microporous material, and dense solid can coexist on the computational grid.
The interface contribution is obtained geometrically from the boundary of a
chosen image phase. The intrinsic contribution is supplied from independent
information, such as finer-resolution imaging or a surface-area measurement,
and weighted by the phase fraction in each cell. Keeping the two contributions
additive allows the image-resolved boundary to be validated separately from
the physical assumption about internal area.

The resulting \texttt{reactiveTransportDBSFoam} formulation is tested through
a validation ladder that separates chemistry--transport conservation,
image-derived interface geometry, and unresolved-area representation. A
one-dimensional analytical benchmark establishes the retardation and inventory
limits. Controlled two- and three-dimensional geometries and image-based
resolved-wall comparators test the interface-area construction. Applications
then examine Pb$^{2+}$ uptake in a fracture network and competitive
Sr$^{2+}$/Ca$^{2+}$ exchange in an Estaillades limestone image. Detailed
protocols, parameter tables, implementation mechanics, and secondary
validation panels are provided in Online Resource~1.

This sequence supports three bounded claims. First, the discrete coupling
recovers a known equilibrium-retardation limit and conserves the combined
aqueous--surface inventory. Second, the reconstructed image-scale interface
area produces integrated area and sorption responses close to body-fitted
references on tractable geometries. Third, the cell-based formulation exposes
spatially heterogeneous uptake in multiscale application images. It does not
establish that an arbitrary intrinsic area is accessible at every transport
rate, nor that a coarse image reproduces all resolved pore-scale dynamics.
