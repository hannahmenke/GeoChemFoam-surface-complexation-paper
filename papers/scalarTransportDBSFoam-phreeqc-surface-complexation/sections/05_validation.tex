\section{Validation}

The validation ladder is organised to separate four distinct questions that
would otherwise be easy to conflate. First, the 1D benchmarks verify the
conservative transport--chemistry coupling independently of image geometry.
Second, controlled geometric tests verify how the reactive surface-area field
$a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ is constructed from segmented images. Third, once that
base geometric construction is established, image-based micromodel comparisons
test the same surface-area construction in reactive transport. Fourth, a
resolved-to-DBS multiscale micromodel benchmark tests whether the upscaled
surface-area representation preserves sorption-controlled transport when part
of the image is treated as an unresolved reactive matrix.
This ordering is important because the intrinsic micropore contribution used in
the later application cases should be interpreted only after the underlying
interface-area and two-scale sorption representations have been assessed.

For the image-derived geometric benchmarks, \texttt{snappyHexMesh} is used as
the resolved-wall reference because it provides an established OpenFOAM route
for constructing a body-fitted mesh from a segmented pore--solid interface.
The explicit wall representation allows reactive area and surface inventory to
be evaluated on a resolved boundary where such a calculation is feasible. It is
used here as a benchmark for the interface-area component of
$a_{\mathrm{surf}}$, not as the target numerical formulation. The snapped mesh
has its own meshing tolerances and wall placement, is expensive to generate and
run for large images, cannot represent unresolved DBS matrix volumes or
intrinsic sub-voxel surface area as a cell-supported inventory, and becomes
unreliable or fails during meshing for the segmented volumes considered here
once the image size is of order \(600^3\) voxels and larger. The validation
therefore asks whether the DBS surface-area fields reproduce the resolved-wall
area distribution and sorption response where an explicit-wall reference can be
generated, while retaining the cell-based representation needed for larger and
multiscale images.

\subsection{1D Analytical Retardation Benchmark}\label{sec:1D}

The 1D linear-retardation benchmark tests the surface-chemistry coupling on a
uniform domain where the retardation factor has an exact analytical form.
With uniform porosity $\theta$ and surface-area density $a_{\mathrm{surf}}$,
the retardation coefficient is
$R = 1 + a_{\mathrm{surf}} K_s / \theta$,
and the retarded concentration front satisfies the standard advection--diffusion
equation with effective velocity $u / (\theta R)$ and effective
diffusivity $D / R$.
This configuration therefore isolates three coupled components of the
implementation: the batch calibration used to extract $K_s$
from two equilibrium states, the
field-level averaging of the surface-capacity term, and the advective--diffusive
transport step. The benchmark compares a surface-enabled transport case, a
matched aqueous-only case (\mbox{$a_{\mathrm{surf}} = 0$}), and a dilute batch
pair used to estimate $K_s$ via the secant method.

For the validated benchmark reported here,
$K_s = 4.253 \times 10^{-6}$ and
$R = 1.851$, so the surface-enabled front travels at
$1/R \approx 54\%$ of the aqueous-only front velocity.
The mesh contains $80 \times 1 \times 1$ cells and uses a species diffusion
coefficient of $10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$.
The surface-enabled profile matches the retarded advection--diffusion reference
with a relative L1-norm error of $1.35\%$ and $L_{\infty}$ error of $2.74\%$.
The aqueous-only profile matches its unretarded reference with L1-norm and
$L_{\infty}$ errors of $1.08\%$ and $5.97\%$, respectively.
The field-level total-equivalent inventory---the domain integral of the combined
aqueous and adsorbed mass---differs from the analytical limit by $1.19\%$ for
the surface-enabled run and by $0.58\%$ for the aqueous-only run.

The solver therefore reproduces the dilute retardation limit of the
DBS formulation at near-percent bulk inventory accuracy. Because the domain
geometry is uniform, any residual error reflects inaccuracies in the
surface-chemistry coupling and the batch-secant estimation procedure rather than
mesh or geometry artefacts. Figure~\ref{fig:1DRetardation} shows the spatial
profiles at $t = 10$, and Figure~\ref{fig:1DBreakthrough} shows the
outlet breakthrough curves over the full run to $t = 80$~s.
The surface-enabled breakthrough arrives at $1/R \approx 54\%$
of the aqueous-only breakthrough time, in agreement with the analytical prediction.
The classical semi-infinite Ogata--Banks solution~\citep{ogata1961solution} is also
shown for comparison and predicts systematically later breakthrough: because the
simulation uses a Neumann (no-flux) outlet, solute cannot diffuse past $x = L$
and accumulates there faster than the semi-infinite formula assumes.
The finite-domain reference, which solves the same 1-D advection--diffusion
equation on $[0, L]$ with a Dirichlet inlet and Neumann outlet matching the
OpenFOAM boundary conditions exactly, captures this effect and gives the
agreement shown.

\subsection{Closed-System Batch Balance}

The chemistry--transport interface is verified against the exact
species-inventory identity for a closed batch domain (no inflow or outflow,
$\mathbf{u} = 0$).
Conservation of the total field-level Ca$^{2+}$ inventory requires
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M} \;=\; \theta\,C + a_{\mathrm{surf}}\,\Gamma \;=\; \mathrm{const},
    \label{eq:closedBalance}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the aqueous concentration, $\Gamma$ is the adsorbed surface concentration, and
$\theta$ and $a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ are the same domain-averaged porosity and
surface-area density as in the 1D transport benchmark.
In the linear dilute limit, Eq.~\eqref{eq:closedBalance} gives the equilibrium
partition
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{\theta\,C_0}{\theta + a_{\mathrm{surf}}\,K_s},
    \label{eq:batchEquil}
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to $C_0 / (1 + a_{\mathrm{surf}}\,K_s/\theta)$.

Two dilute initial concentrations are tested on the
80\,\texttimes\,1\,\texttimes\,1 mesh with
$\theta = 0.5$ and $a_{\mathrm{surf}} = 10^5\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ using the same
Ca$^{2+}$--\texttt{Surf\_a} surface chemistry as in Section~\ref{sec:1D}.
The domains are initialised at $C_0=10^{-9}$ and
$2\times10^{-9}$~mol\,L$^{-1}$ with the surface equilibrated against the
specified initial solution. A single PHREEQC call then partitions Ca$^{2+}$
between the pore fluid and surface.
The relative inventory drift
$|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{end}} - \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{start}}| / \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{start}}$
is $8.9 \times 10^{-10}$ and $1.1 \times 10^{-10}$, respectively.
This confirms that the chemistry--transport interface conserves the total
field-level Ca$^{2+}$ inventory to the PHREEQC convergence level over the
tested dilute range.

\subsection{Controlled Surface-Area Validation}

The first geometric validation step isolates the construction of
$a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ from transport and chemistry by using analytic curved
interfaces. Two cases are used so that both dimensional branches of
\texttt{smoothSurface} are tested. The 3D branch is evaluated on a voxelized
sphere with reference area $4\pi R^2$; the quasi-2D branch is evaluated on a
circular post with reference area $2\pi R h$, where $h$ is the out-of-plane
thickness. These benchmarks ask a narrow question: which image-based
surface-area construction is closest to the intended smooth interface?

The smooth-interface reconstruction recovers a sub-voxel interface before
mapping its area back into the DBS cells. For the 3D sphere, the
\texttt{smoothSurface} reconstruction gives errors of $-0.06\%$ and
$+0.87\%$ at the same resolutions, comparable to the body-fitted
\texttt{snappyHexMesh} reference ($-0.65\%$ and $-0.50\%$, respectively).
For the 2D circular post, the \texttt{smoothSurface} contour-length
construction remains within
$0.2\%$ of the analytic value across the tested resolutions. The corresponding
\texttt{snappyHexMesh} circular-cylinder reference is also sub-percent, with
the largest error below $0.1\%$. The controlled benchmarks therefore show that
\texttt{smoothSurface} materially improves the geometric fidelity of
$a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ for both the 3D isosurface route and the quasi-2D contour
route used in the micromodel validation.

\subsection{Micromodel Image-Based Surface-Area Validation}

The second geometric validation step moves from the ideal sphere and circular
post to an image-based reactive benchmark while retaining the focus on the
interface-area construction. The solid-grain micromodel benchmark compares
two representations of the same segmented geometry: the resolved-wall
\texttt{snappyHexMesh} reference and DBS \texttt{smoothSurface}. This
comparison tests whether the cell-based geometric
surface-area reconstruction also improves reactive-transport predictions on a
real image-based domain.

The resolved-wall and DBS results represent the image-derived geometry with
different numerical objects. In the \texttt{snappyHexMesh} case, the geometry
is a body-fitted wall and its face area is mapped to the adjacent chemistry
cells as an explicit surface-area density. In DBS, the same area is obtained
from the image-based smooth-interface reconstruction. Surface complexation in
both cases therefore samples a cell-supported area density together with the
local aqueous concentration. The pressure drops were calibrated separately to
match the inlet volumetric flux. The appropriate comparison is not exact wall
placement, but the
integrated reactive area, its spatial distribution, the aqueous transport
profile, and the resulting sorbed inventory.

For the resolved-wall \texttt{snappyHexMesh} reference, the integrated
reactive area is $1.65 \times 10^{-5}$~m$^2$, compared with
$1.67 \times 10^{-5}$~m$^2$ for DBS \texttt{smoothSurface}, an error of
$+1.48\%$. The separately calibrated pressure drops give inlet volumetric
fluxes that agree within $0.004\%$. At $t=200$~s, the dissolved inventory
differs by $-0.07\%$ and the streamwise aqueous-profile L1 error is $0.48\%$.
The DBS sorbed inventory differs by $+1.68\%$. The close agreement across
integrated area, aqueous transport, and sorbed inventory shows that the
cell-based smooth-interface representation reproduces the resolved-wall
surface-complexation response on this image-based geometry.

\subsection{3D External Surface-Area and Transport Validation}

A third validation step tests the same resolved-wall/DBS comparison in a
three-dimensional image crop. A $128^3$-voxel Estaillades subvolume is used,
with the micropore label remapped to solid so that the comparison isolates the
external pore--grain surface. The two cases use the same
Sr$^{2+}$/Ca$^{2+}$ surface-complexation chemistry and separately calibrated
pressure drops to match the Darcy velocity: a resolved-wall
\texttt{snappyHexMesh} reference and DBS \texttt{smoothSurface}. The
reactive-transport comparison is made at
$t = 2.5$~s, for which the advective front has traversed approximately half
of the crop under the resolved-wall Darcy velocity. For this external-surface
validation, the \texttt{smoothSurface} Gaussian occupancy width is
$\sigma = 0.74$ voxels.

The 3D benchmark separates three quantities that affect the reactive result:
the integrated external area, the DBS flow field, and the local overlap between
aqueous Sr$^{2+}$ and reactive surface area. The calibrated Darcy velocities
agree within $1.12\%$. With \texttt{smoothSurface}, the integrated reactive
area is $1.52\%$ greater than the resolved-wall value and the sorbed Sr$^{2+}$
inventory is $1.84\%$ lower. This agreement in both integrated area and sorbed
inventory shows that the cell-based reconstruction preserves the external-
surface-complexation response in the three-dimensional image crop.

\subsection{Resolved-to-DBS Two-Scale Sorption Benchmark}

The unresolved-matrix component of the DBS model is tested with an aligned
resolved-to-upscaled micromodel benchmark based on the multiscale images used in
the dispersivity study of \citet{maes2024dispersivity}. The resolved reference
uses a high-resolution crop of the micromodel in which the matrix pore space is
explicitly represented. The DBS calculation uses the corresponding lower
resolution image of the same physical region, in which the large vugs remain
resolved while the matrix phase is assigned porosity and intrinsic reactive
surface area from the high-resolution matrix representative elementary volume.

Figure~\ref{fig:twoScaleLithiumNestedGeometries} shows the aligned geometry
hierarchy used for this comparison. The upscaled DBS and resolved-reference
transport domains have the same physical size, while the matrix representative
elementary volume provides the unresolved matrix properties used in the DBS
calculation.

The reactive-transport problem is formulated as a critical-mineral recovery
analogue for direct lithium extraction. A Li-bearing Na--K--Mg brine invades an
initially Li-free brine, and Li$^{+}$ competes with the major brine cations for
a generic sorbent site:
\[
    \mathrm{Sor\_aH} + \mathrm{Li}^{+}
        \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Sor\_aLi} + \mathrm{H}^{+}.
\]
Parallel reactions for Na$^{+}$, K$^{+}$, and Mg$^{2+}$ provide competing
surface occupancy. The chemistry is intentionally generic: it represents a
Li-selective surface-complexation benchmark for engineered sorbent coatings,
not a parameterisation of a specific commercial direct-lithium-extraction
material.

The resolved and DBS cases are simulated at matched pore-scale P\'{e}clet number,
\[
    Pe = \frac{U_D L_{\mathrm{pore}}}{D\theta},
\]
where $U_D$ is the Darcy velocity, $L_{\mathrm{pore}}$ is the pore length scale
reported by the flow post-processing, $D$ is the aqueous diffusion coefficient,
and $\theta$ is the porosity. This matching is required because the resolved
and DBS images have different permeability and pore-length scales; equal
pressure gradients would therefore compare different transport regimes. The
benchmark evaluates the DBS representation by comparing the spatial
Li$^{+}$ aqueous concentration and sorbed \(\mathrm{Sor\_aLi}\) inventories
against the fully resolved reference at the same $Pe$.

The P\'{e}clet number is also expected to control the accuracy of the
two-scale reduction. At low $Pe$, diffusive exchange can equilibrate the
unresolved matrix over the residence time of the advecting fluid, so the DBS
cell behaves as an effective local reservoir with averaged porosity, diffusion
coefficient, permeability, and intrinsic surface area. At higher $Pe$, solute
can bypass part of the matrix before diffusion has accessed the internal
surface. In that regime, matching the integrated surface area is not sufficient:
the reactive prediction also depends on the time-dependent accessibility of
that area to the aqueous front. The resolved-to-DBS benchmark is therefore
interpreted as a test of Pe-dependent surface accessibility, rather than as a
pure comparison of total surface area.

The production sweep covers $Pe=0.1$, 1, 3, and 10. At matched
$x/L=0.625$, the largest aqueous and sorbed inventory differences are
$0.84\%$ and $0.40\%$, respectively, while the largest streamwise-profile L1
errors are $1.94\%$ and $5.73\%$. Map dissimilarities remain below
$2.1\times10^{-3}$ for both inventories across the sweep
(Figure~\ref{fig:twoScaleErrorVsPe}). These results lie within the predefined
$5\%$ integrated-inventory, $10\%$ profile-L1, and 0.1 map-dissimilarity
acceptance limits. The weak increase in map dissimilarity with $Pe$ is
consistent with progressively less complete diffusive access to the
unresolved matrix, but it is small relative to the acceptance threshold.

\subsection{Validation Summary}\label{sec:valSummary}

The validation sequence therefore separates conservative chemistry--transport
coupling, image-derived surface geometry, and two-scale DBS sorption
upscaling. The 1D analytical and closed-system checks verify that the
transport--chemistry coupling conserves inventory and reproduces the expected
retardation limit on a domain with no geometric ambiguity. The controlled
sphere and circular-post benchmarks isolate the geometric accuracy of the
interface-area construction itself. The micromodel solid-grain and 3D
external-surface comparisons transfer that geometric question to reactive image
benchmarks by comparing DBS \texttt{smoothSurface} with resolved-wall
\texttt{snappyHexMesh} references.
The resolved-to-DBS micromodel benchmark tests the two-scale reactive-transport
representation used when part of an image is treated as an unresolved reactive
matrix; its production $Pe$ sweep satisfies the predefined inventory,
streamwise-profile, and map-overlap criteria. Table~\ref{tab:validationSummary} summarises the 1D
benchmark family; the geometric validation results are reported in
Figures~\ref{fig:sphereSurfaceValidation}, \ref{fig:hm12SurfaceValidation},
and~\ref{fig:external3DSurfaceValidation}, with global errors in
Tables~\ref{tab:micromodelProfileErrors} and~\ref{tab:external3DValidation};
the two-scale transport results are reported in
Figures~\ref{fig:twoScaleErrorVsPe} and~\ref{fig:twoScalePeOneFields}.

A consistent pattern emerges across the 1D sensitivity studies: local
(cell-level) drift reaches $3$--$4.5\%$ while bulk (volume-integrated) drift
stays below $0.6\%$.
This disparity indicates that operator-splitting and timestep errors are
concentrated near the chemical front and cancel almost completely at the domain
level, so global conservation properties are preserved even where local
truncation errors are non-negligible.
The near-zero front-position drift ($2.5 \times 10^{-7}$) from the mesh study
confirms that the retardation-front position is not systematically biased by
mesh resolution.
The validation results therefore support the conservative coupling
and the smooth-interface construction of $a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ used in the
application cases that follow. Those cases then examine the additional
intrinsic micropore contribution in progressively richer image-based media.
