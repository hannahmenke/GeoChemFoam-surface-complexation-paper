\section{Model Description}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Mobile Water and Reactive Area}

The DBS micro-continuum formulation represents the image as cell-centred
material fields. The porosity $\epsilon$ is the mobile-water volume per bulk
cell volume and is denoted $\theta$ in the analytical expressions; hence
$\theta=\epsilon$ in every reported case. Flow obeys
\begin{align}
\nabla\!\cdot\bm{u}_D &= 0,
\label{eq:continuity}\\
-\nabla p+\nabla\!\cdot(\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}\nabla\bm{u})
-\frac{\mu}{K(\epsilon)}\bm{u}_D&=\bm{0},
\label{eq:brinkman}
\end{align}
where $\bm{u}_D=\epsilon\bm{u}$ is the Darcy velocity and the permeability
$K(\epsilon)$ recovers Stokes flow in open pores while suppressing velocity in
solid-like cells \citep{brinkman1949calculation,soulaine2016micro}.
Open-pore cells approach $\theta=1$, microporous cells retain an intermediate
mobile-water fraction, and near-solid cells approach zero. In the present
implementation the same fixed $\theta$ is used by transport and PHREEQC; no
second porosity is introduced during the chemical update. This common water
volume is distinct from the independent reactive-area field below.

Reactive area is a separate cell property. Its bulk-volume density
$a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ (m$^2$\,m$^{-3}_{\mathrm{bulk}}$) combines the visible
boundary of a designated image phase with area internal to an unresolved
phase:
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{surf}}=a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{interface}}
+a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{intrinsic}}.
\label{eq:asurf}
\end{equation}
The interface term is reconstructed at sub-voxel resolution from the
segmented occupancy and mapped back to the owning finite-volume cells. The
intrinsic term assigns a measured or independently estimated surface density
$s_j$ to each material label $j$ and weights it by the local phase fraction
$f_j$:
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{intrinsic}}=\sum_j f_j s_j.
\label{eq:asurf_intrinsic}
\end{equation}
Thus a phase can contribute both its image-scale outer boundary and its
sub-voxel internal area. Both fields are fixed during the calculations
reported here. Online Resource~1 gives the reconstruction protocol and the
two- and three-dimensional geometric checks.

The two terms describe different physical scales. The interface term measures
the outer boundary visible in the segmented image; smoothing and sub-voxel
reconstruction reduce the staircase bias of a face-counted voxel boundary. The
intrinsic term represents pore walls or roughness wholly contained within an
image phase and cannot be inferred from that outer boundary. A value reported
per microporous-phase volume or grain mass must consequently be converted to
bulk volume before entering Eq.~\eqref{eq:asurf_intrinsic}. Examples include
nano-CT or FIB-SEM estimates of matrix area and gas-adsorption measurements
combined with phase fraction and density. The formulation accepts these inputs
but does not prescribe their experimental upscaling.

This distinction is consequential in a multiscale image. A coarse microporous
cell can have a low mobile-water fraction, finite effective diffusivity, and a
large surface capacity. Treating its internal area as a pore--grain boundary
would require an artificial wall position and would obscure the volume over
which the aqueous concentration is defined. Treating the same uptake as an
unspecified volumetric reaction would, conversely, lose the surface inventory
needed to evaluate adsorption and exchange. The cell field retains both
quantities: DBS controls delivery through the mobile water, and the PHREEQC
surface state stores the amount associated with the independently specified
area.

PhreeqcRM uses a representative-bulk-volume convention. The field is therefore
converted from cubic metres to litres without porosity rescaling:
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{surf}}^{\mathrm{bulk,L}}=a_{\mathrm{surf}}/10^3.
\label{eq:asurf_bulk_litre}
\end{equation}
For surface master $k$, multiplying $a_{\mathrm{surf}}$ by its site density
(mol\,m$^{-2}$) defines the reactive capacity per bulk volume. This mapping
keeps water storage, area, and surface capacity dimensionally distinct.
If $a_{\mathrm{surf}}=0$, the surface contribution vanishes without changing
the aqueous balance. Conversely, increasing area changes chemical capacity but
does not increase mobile-water storage or advective flux.

\subsection{Conservative Transport and Surface Inventory}

For each aqueous species $i$ with concentration $C_i$, transport is advanced
as
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial(\theta C_i)}{\partial t}
+\nabla\!\cdot(\bm{u}_D C_i)
-\nabla\!\cdot(\theta D_i\nabla C_i)=0,
\label{eq:transport}
\end{equation}
where $D_i$ is the molecular diffusivity in open pores and an effective
diffusivity in microporous cells. The factor $\theta$ accounts once for the
mobile-water volume. Chemistry is applied after transport rather than through
an explicit source term.

For a microporous phase, $D_i$ may be written as
$D_i^{\mathrm{mol}}/\tau$, with tortuosity $\tau\geq1$; the prefactor
$\theta$ still accounts for reduced water volume. This separation prevents
porosity from being folded twice into diffusion. The flux is evaluated on cell
faces, while the accumulation and chemical inventories remain cell-centred.

The formulation consequently has a simple aqueous-only limit. If the area
field or site density is zero, PHREEQC contributes no surface capacity and the
transport equation reduces to the conservative DBS aqueous balance. If area is
present but the chemistry mask excludes a near-solid cell, both the local
solution and its surface contribution are excluded together. This avoids a
surface reservoir with no meaningful aqueous volume, while preserving the
separate roles of porosity and area in all active cells.

The corresponding local inventory for one aqueous/surface pair is
\begin{equation}
M=\theta C+a_{\mathrm{surf}}\Gamma,
\label{eq:inventory}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma$ is the surface concentration (mol\,m$^{-2}$). The aqueous and
adsorbed terms are consequently expressed on the same bulk-volume basis.
Multi-species inventories sum the relevant aqueous and adsorbed components.
For a closed domain, the volume integral of Eq.~\eqref{eq:inventory} should be
constant to discretisation and chemistry tolerances. In a flow-through domain,
its change must equal the net boundary flux. This quantity is the diagnostic
used in the analytical and batch tests rather than aqueous concentration alone.

The inventory is also useful when interpreting competitive systems. For an ion
that exchanges with a resident species, a decrease in aqueous concentration
alone can result from dilution, transport, or adsorption. A simultaneous change
in the corresponding surface inventory identifies the transfer to the reactive
phase. For a closed batch, the summed inventory isolates chemistry conservation;
for a flow-through domain, it provides the state variable against which inlet
and outlet fluxes must be balanced. This is why the reported comparisons retain
both dissolved and adsorbed quantities.

The solver applies first-order Lie splitting \citep{carrayrou2004operator}:
\begin{align}
\widetilde{\bm C}^{,n+1}&=\mathcal T_{\Delta t}
(\bm C^n,\theta,\bm u_D,\bm D),\\
(\bm C^{n+1},\bm\Gamma^{n+1})&=\mathcal R_{\Delta t}
(\widetilde{\bm C}^{,n+1},\bm\Gamma^n,\theta,a_{\mathrm{surf}}),
\label{eq:split}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal T$ is the conservative finite-volume operator and
$\mathcal R$ is the cell-local PHREEQC equilibrium operator. Surface reactions
are assumed to equilibrate faster than transport over one timestep; kinetic
surface reactions are outside the present scope.

Lie splitting has global first-order error in $\Delta t$ and local error of
order $\Delta t^2$ \citep{carrayrou2004operator}. It is consistent with the
first-order temporal and advective discretisation used here, but its error can
grow when chemistry is stiff or when transport and reaction fronts evolve on
similar timescales. The present validation therefore tests the coupled update
at the reported timesteps rather than treating equilibrium chemistry alone as
sufficient evidence of numerical accuracy.

\subsection{Analytical Dilute Limit}

In the dilute linear-equilibrium limit, $\Gamma=K_sC$, where the Henry
coefficient $K_s$ (m) is obtained from two batch equilibrations as
$\Delta\Gamma/\Delta C$. Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:inventory} gives
\begin{equation}
R=1+\frac{a_{\mathrm{surf}}K_s}{\theta}.
\label{eq:retardation}
\end{equation}
The analytical advection--diffusion solution then uses velocity
$u_D/(\theta R)$ and diffusivity $D/R$ \citep{ogata1961solution}. This link
tests both the area-to-capacity mapping and conservative exchange between the
two terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:inventory}; its derivation and finite-domain boundary
conditions are given in Online Resource~1.

Because $K_s$ is extracted from the same PHREEQC surface model used by the
transport calculation, the benchmark avoids an independent database-unit
conversion. It tests whether the calibrated chemical slope is transferred
through area and porosity into the predicted front delay. The dilute limit is
not used as an approximation for the nonlinear competitive applications.
